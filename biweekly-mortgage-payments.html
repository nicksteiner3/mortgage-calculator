Biweekly Mortgage Payments: Do They Save Money?

Paying half your mortgage every two weeks makes 26 half-payments a year, which equals 13 full monthly payments. That extra payment can cut years off a 30-year loan.

How the savings work

A standard 30-year loan has 12 monthly payments a year. A biweekly plan has 26 half-payments, so you pay one extra full payment each year. That extra money goes straight to principal, which lowers the balance faster and reduces the interest charged.

In a simplified model of a $320,000 loan at 6.5% over 30 years, adding one-twelfth of the $2,022.62 payment to each month's payment pays the loan off in about 24.2 years and saves roughly $93,000 in interest. Your exact result depends on your rate, balance, and how your servicer applies each payment.

Check before you sign up

Some servicers charge a fee for biweekly enrollment, and some third-party programs take a fee to set it up. You can get the same result for free by paying one extra full payment each year, or by adding one-twelfth of your monthly payment to each monthly payment. Confirm that extra money is applied to principal, not held until the next due date.

Why 26 half-payments equal 13 full payments

A year has 52 weeks, which is 26 two-week periods. A standard monthly plan makes 12 payments a year, but a biweekly plan makes 26 half-payments. Those half-payments add up to 13 full payments, so you make one extra full payment each year without noticing it in any single paycheck.

The extra money is applied to principal, which reduces the balance faster. A smaller balance means less interest accrues each month, and the payoff date moves earlier. The savings come from that extra principal, not from the schedule alone.

A worked example

Take the same loan used elsewhere on this site: $320,000 at 6.5% for 30 years, with a standard monthly payment of $2,022.62. If you paid one-twelfth of that payment extra each month, you would send about $168.55 more per month, or roughly $2,022.62 total each year beyond the regular schedule.

Check fees and how payments are applied

Biweekly programs come in several forms. Some servicers offer a free biweekly option, while some third-party companies charge a setup fee and sometimes a per-payment fee. Before you enroll, ask whether the program is free and how it is handled. Many lenders will simply accept extra principal payments without any enrollment.

Confirm how partial payments are held. Some servicers keep a partial payment in a suspense account until the next full installment arrives. In that case, your balance does not drop until the full payment posts, and the interest savings may be smaller. Ask for written confirmation of how your payments are processed, and check your statements to make sure each payment reduces principal as expected.

Do it yourself instead

You can get the same effect without a formal biweekly program. Choose one of these methods:

  • Pay one-twelfth of your monthly principal and interest payment with each regular payment.
  • Make one extra full principal payment each year, such as with a bonus or tax refund.
  • Round up your monthly payment to a convenient amount and mark the extra as principal.

These approaches give you control over timing. If your income changes, you can pause the extra payments without changing your required loan terms.

Is biweekly right for your budget?

Biweekly payments can help if your paychecks arrive every two weeks and you want to match payments to income. They can also make it easier to save if the extra money would otherwise be spent. However, the savings depend on making every payment on time and paying the extra amount consistently.

Before committing, make sure you have an emergency fund and can cover your required payment during a slow month. If a biweekly plan would leave you short on other bills, a smaller extra payment or a monthly approach may be a better fit. Any money you put toward the mortgage is also money you can't use for savings or retirement, so compare the options.
