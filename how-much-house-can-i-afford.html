How Much House Can I Afford?

Two common rules of thumb give a quick starting range. Lenders may approve you for more, but these rules keep the payment comfortable.

The 28% rule

Keep your total housing cost (principal, interest, taxes, and insurance) under 28% of your gross monthly income.

The 36% rule

Keep all of your monthly debt payments, including housing, under 36% of your gross monthly income.

Example: on $100,000 a year ($8,333 a month), 28% allows about $2,333 for housing, and 36% allows about $3,000 for all debt payments.

Start with gross monthly income

Both rules use gross income, meaning the amount before taxes and payroll deductions. To find it, divide your yearly gross pay by 12. If you're paid hourly, multiply your hourly rate by your average weekly hours and then by 52, and divide by 12. If your income varies, use a conservative average from the last two years rather than your best year.

Lenders also look at your gross income, so your numbers here should match what you'd report on a loan application.

What the 28% and 36% rules measure

The 28% rule, sometimes called the front-end ratio, limits your total housing cost. Housing cost includes principal, interest, property tax, and home insurance, and often PMI and HOA dues. The 36% rule, the back-end ratio, adds all of your monthly debt payments, such as car loans, student loans, credit card minimums, and child support, to the housing cost.

To use both rules, calculate the most you can spend on housing under each one and use the lower figure. For example, with $8,333 in monthly gross income (a $100,000 yearly salary), 28% is about $2,333 for housing. Under the 36% rule, the total is about $3,000 for all debts.

A worked example with existing debt

Suppose the same $8,333 monthly income comes with a $500 car payment and a $200 student loan payment, for $700 in other debt each month. The 36% limit of $3,000 minus $700 leaves $2,300 for housing. The 28% limit is $2,333. Because $2,300 is lower, it is the cap to use.

In this case, the debt payments reduce the price you can afford, even though the 28% rule alone would allow a bit more. Paying down a car loan before buying a home can therefore increase your buying power.

Turning a monthly budget into a home price

Suppose your housing budget is $2,300 per month. Property tax and insurance in the calculator default are $525 per month combined, which leaves about $1,775 for principal and interest. At 6.5% over 30 years, that payment supports a loan of roughly $280,800. With 20% down, the home price would be about $351,000. Using different rates, terms, taxes, and insurance will change the answer, and PMI or HOA dues would lower it further.

Costs the rules of thumb can miss

  • Down payment and closing costs. These are upfront cash needs that the monthly ratios do not show.
  • Maintenance and repairs. Roofs, furnaces, and appliances need money over time, and older homes often need more.
  • Utilities and commuting. A home farther from work may come with higher transportation costs.
  • Irregular expenses. Childcare, medical costs, and savings goals do not appear in the ratios.

Lenders can approve loans at higher debt ratios under some programs. A higher approved amount does not mean the payment will feel comfortable, so set your own limit with your full budget in mind.

Next steps

Gather your pay stubs, tax returns, and a list of monthly debts. Get a preapproval letter from a lender to see the rate and loan amount they can offer. Compare that amount with the results of these rules. The lower of the two is usually the safer target for a monthly budget.

Frequently asked questions

Is 28% and 36% a rule lenders must follow? No. They are common guidelines. Lenders set their own limits, which can be higher or lower depending on the loan and your credit profile.

Should I use my gross or take-home pay? Use gross pay, the amount before taxes and deductions, because lenders calculate ratios that way.

What if I'm self-employed? Lenders usually average your net business income over a period of two years or more, so your qualifying income may differ from what you earn in a strong year.

General guidance only, not financial advice. Your lender's approval limits and your own budget matter more.
