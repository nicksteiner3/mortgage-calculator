What Is PMI on a Mortgage and How to Remove It

Private mortgage insurance (PMI) protects the lender if you borrow more than 80% of the home's value. It's usually added to your monthly payment.

When you pay PMI

Most conventional loans require PMI when your down payment is less than 20%. The cost is typically a percentage of the loan amount each year, and it depends on your credit score and down payment.

How to get rid of it

Under federal law, your lender must cancel PMI automatically when your balance reaches 78% of the original home value, if you're current on payments. You can ask to cancel it earlier at 80% of the original value, or when the home's current value supports it. Keep records of your payments and any appraisal you use.

What PMI costs

PMI premiums are usually expressed as a yearly percentage of the loan amount. The percentage depends on your credit score, loan size, loan-to-value ratio (how much you borrow compared with the home's value), and the type of PMI. Because quotes vary, treat any figure here as an illustration only.

As an example, imagine a $320,000 loan with an assumed PMI cost of 0.5% per year. That would be $1,600 per year, or about $133 per month, added to your principal and interest payment. A lower-cost quote or a higher down payment would reduce that amount, and a weaker credit profile could increase it.

PMI is often included in the monthly payment through escrow, so the total you see on your statement is larger than the principal and interest figure alone. Ask your lender to show PMI as a separate line on the Loan Estimate.

Ways PMI can be paid

  • Monthly premiums. The most common option. The cost is added to each payment and can be removed later under the rules described below.
  • Upfront premium. Some policies are paid in one lump sum at closing or financed into the loan. This lowers the monthly payment but adds cash needed at purchase.
  • Lender-paid PMI. The lender pays the insurance and recovers the cost through a higher interest rate. This removes the monthly premium line, but the higher rate usually lasts for the life of the loan unless you refinance.
  • Split premiums. A combination of an upfront payment and smaller monthly charges.

Each structure has tradeoffs, so compare the total cost over the years you expect to keep the loan, not just the first-year payment.

When PMI can be removed

For most conventional loans, federal law gives you two main paths. You can ask your servicer to cancel PMI once your loan balance is scheduled to reach 80% of the home's original value. Your servicer must cancel it automatically when the balance is scheduled to reach 78% of the original value, provided you are current on payments. Lenders often also require a clean payment history and may ask for an appraisal before canceling based on a higher home value.

To request cancellation early, send a written request to your servicer and ask what documents they need. Check whether the home must be your primary residence and whether any second liens are in place, since those can affect eligibility. Keep copies of your payment history, appraisal reports, and all correspondence.

Other ways to get rid of PMI

If you've built equity through rising home values or extra payments, a new appraisal may show you have enough equity to request cancellation. Making improvements that raise the home's value can help as well, though the lender decides what counts. Refinancing into a new loan can remove PMI if the new loan-to-value ratio is low enough, but refinancing has closing costs and may change your rate, so compare the total cost.

PMI rules are different for FHA loans. FHA loans use mortgage insurance premiums (MIP) with their own cancellation rules, and on many FHA loans with less than 10% down, the premium lasts for the life of the loan. Check the terms of your specific loan before assuming PMI will end.

How PMI affects your budget

Before choosing a home, add the estimated PMI to your principal, interest, tax, and insurance figures. Using the example above, $133 per month of PMI would raise a payment of about $2,022.62 to roughly $2,156 before tax and insurance. Remember that PMI is a cost of borrowing with a small down payment, not a fee that builds equity for you.

If PMI would push your payment beyond what you can comfortably afford, consider a larger down payment, a lower price, or a different loan program. Compare several Loan Estimates to see how PMI and rate interact.
