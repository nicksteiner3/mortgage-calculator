Run your own numbers

How extra principal payments work

Each month, your lender charges interest on the balance you still owe. Your regular payment covers that interest first and the rest reduces principal. When you pay extra and tell your lender to apply it to principal, the balance drops sooner. The next month's interest is then calculated on a smaller amount, and that effect compounds over the life of the loan.

Extra payments don't change your required monthly payment unless you ask for a recast or refinance. Instead, they shorten the loan, because you reach a zero balance before the originally scheduled date.

Ways to add extra money

There are several common approaches, and each one works the same way once the money reaches principal:

  • A fixed monthly amount. Adding a set amount, such as $100 or $200, to each payment is simple and easy to budget.
  • One extra full payment per year. Some borrowers send an extra payment in a bonus or tax-refund month.
  • Lump sums. Windfalls like a raise, inheritance, or sale of another asset can go to principal in one step.
  • Biweekly schedules. These spread the payment over 26 half-payments a year. See the biweekly page for how that compares.

Whichever method you choose, keep it steady. Consistent payments are easier to maintain than occasional large ones, and a fixed amount is easy to cancel if money gets tight.

Making sure the money reaches principal

Extra money sent without instructions may be treated as an early payment for next month's bill. Some servicers hold it in a separate account until a full payment is available. In either case, it may not reduce your balance right away.

Before you start, contact your loan servicer and ask three questions. First, how do they apply extra payments? Second, is there a specific field or form for principal-only payments? Third, do they charge any fee for prepayment? Prepayment penalties are uncommon on standard mortgages, but you should confirm that your loan does not have one. Keep a record of each extra payment and check that your next statement shows the principal reduction.

Extra payments versus other goals

Paying down the mortgage is only one use for spare cash. Before making extra payments, consider the following:

  • Emergency savings. A cash reserve can cover job loss or repairs without new borrowing. Money locked in home equity is hard to access quickly.
  • Higher-interest debt. Credit card and some personal loan balances often charge more than a mortgage. Paying those first is usually the better use of money.
  • Employer retirement matches. If your employer matches retirement contributions, capturing the full match is often worth more than extra mortgage payments.
  • Your mortgage rate. A lower rate means the interest saved from prepaying is smaller. Compare the rate with what you could earn elsewhere.

A simple plan to get started

Choose an amount you can keep paying for at least a year. Set up the extra payment as a separate, principal-only transaction. Check your loan balance after the first few payments to confirm the reduction. Review the plan once a year, especially if your income or other debts change. If you ever need the cash back, you can stop the extra payments; the loan remains on its original schedule with the same required payment.
