15 vs 30 Year Mortgage: Which Costs Less?

A 15-year mortgage has a higher monthly payment but much less total interest. A 30-year mortgage has a lower monthly payment and costs more over time. Here's a worked example.

Example: $320,000 loan at 6.5%

15-year30-year
Monthly principal and interest$2,787.54$2,022.62
Total interest over the loan$181,758$408,142

The 15-year loan costs about $765 more per month but saves about $226,000 in interest. Your own rate and fees will change these numbers.

Which should you choose?

Choose a 15-year loan if you can comfortably afford the higher payment and want to pay off the home sooner. Choose a 30-year loan if you need the lower monthly payment or want to keep cash available for other goals. A 30-year loan lets you make extra payments when you can, while still keeping the required payment low.

How the two loans differ

Both loans borrow the same amount and charge the same interest rate, but they spread repayment over different periods. A 15-year loan has 180 monthly payments. A 30-year loan has 360. Because the balance is repaid in half the time, each payment on the shorter loan must be much larger.

The monthly payment uses the formula M = P × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), where P is the loan amount, r is the annual rate divided by 12, and n is the number of payments. Changing n from 360 to 180 has a big effect on the result, which is why the 15-year payment in the example is about $765 higher.

Lenders often price 15-year loans at a different rate than 30-year loans. That rate can be lower or higher depending on the market and your profile, so compare actual quotes for each term rather than assuming one.

Why the shorter loan costs less interest

Interest is charged each month on the balance you still owe. On the 15-year loan, the balance falls faster, so less interest builds up over the life of the loan. On the 30-year loan, the balance stays high for longer, and interest keeps accruing on a larger amount.

In the example, the 30-year loan pays about $408,000 in interest, while the 15-year loan pays about $182,000. The gap of roughly $226,000 is the cost of stretching the loan out, even though the 30-year payment is lower each month.

Testing the monthly budget

Before choosing the 15-year loan, add the higher payment to your full monthly budget. Include property tax, home insurance, any PMI, and an allowance for repairs. A useful check is whether you could still make the payment comfortably if your income dropped or an unexpected expense came up.

The 30-year loan gives you flexibility. If you choose it, you can still make extra payments whenever your budget allows. For example, an extra $200 per month on a $320,000 loan at 6.5% would shorten the term to about 23.4 years and save roughly $105,000 in interest, according to the same standard formula. Your lender must apply the extra money to principal for that to happen, so confirm how payments are handled.

The required payment on a 30-year loan is lower, which can help if your income varies, but the total interest is higher. Each choice trades monthly cash flow against lifetime cost.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Choosing the largest loan you qualify for, which can leave little room in the budget.
  • Comparing only monthly payments and ignoring total interest.
  • Skipping retirement savings or an emergency fund to afford a higher payment.
  • Forgetting that tax, insurance, and PMI are added to the principal and interest payment.

Work through the numbers for both terms with your own price, down payment, and quoted rate. A calculator can show the math, but your lender's written estimate will give the exact figures.

Frequently asked questions

Can I refinance later from a 30-year to a 15-year loan? Yes, if your finances and the market allow it. A refinance has closing costs and a new rate, so compare the total cost before switching.

Does a 15-year loan always have a lower rate? Not always. Rates depend on the lender and the market, so compare quotes for both terms.

Is the required payment on a 30-year loan the most I have to pay? No. The required payment is the minimum. You can pay more at any time, subject to your loan terms.
